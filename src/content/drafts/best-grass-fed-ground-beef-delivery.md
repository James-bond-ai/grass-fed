Grass-fed ground beef sold by mail cost about $12 to $15 a pound in 1 pound packs when we checked five sellers' websites on November 20, 2026, and about $10 to $15.50 a pound in bulk bundles. The cheapest per-pound option was a 25 pound bundle of 75% lean beef on sale, and leaner grinds cost more at every seller that offered a choice. Before you compare prices, check two things on the label: the lean/fat ratio and whether the word is "ground beef" or "hamburger."

## Ground beef vs hamburger: what the federal rules say

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) sets standards for what can be sold under each name. Under federal rule 9 CFR 319.15:

- **Ground beef** (or chopped beef) can contain at most 30% fat. No beef fat can be added to it as a separate ingredient, and no water, phosphates, binders or extenders are allowed. Seasoning is allowed.
- **Hamburger** has the same 30% fat cap and the same ban on water and binders, but beef fat can be added to reach the fat level.

So both names cap fat at 30%. The difference is where the fat comes from: in ground beef it comes with the trimmings that were ground, while in hamburger extra fat can be added. Almost every grass-fed seller we looked at uses "ground beef," but if you see "hamburger" on a farm's price list, that is the difference.

Ground beef that has heart, liver or kidney mixed in is a different product with its own name. US Wellness Meats, for example, sells an "Organ Blend" bundle of 75% lean ground beef with heart, kidney and liver. The name on the package tells you what is in it.

## How to read "85% lean / 15% fat"

The two numbers are the share of lean meat and the share of fat by weight. 85/15 means 85% lean and 15% fat. A lower first number means more fat.

The rules for printing those numbers come from 9 CFR 317.362 and FSIS guidance:

- A label can state a lean percentage (such as "85% lean") on ground beef that does not qualify as "low fat," but only if the fat percentage appears right next to it in the same size, color and type of lettering.
- FSIS's nutrition labeling Q&A (last updated April 2012) says a shorthand like "85/15" by itself is not acceptable on a retail label. It has to read along the lines of "85% lean/15% fat."
- The same Q&A says the lean/fat statement is voluntary.
- "Lean" and "extra lean" are defined terms. Per 100 grams, "lean" means under 10 grams of fat and "extra lean" means under 5 grams, with limits on saturated fat and cholesterol too. A product called "lean ground beef" has to meet that definition.

Most mail-order sellers use the short form (80/20, 90/10) on their websites. That is fine for a product page. The package itself should carry the full wording.

### Why a small farm's label may say less

Ground beef normally has to carry nutrition information, but the FSIS Q&A describes a small business exemption: companies with 500 or fewer employees that make under 100,000 pounds a year of a product, and put no nutrition claims on it, may not need a Nutrition Facts panel. Since the lean/fat statement is voluntary, a small farm's ground beef can be labeled simply "Ground Beef" with no ratio at all.

If you are buying from a local farm or a small ranch that ships, ask what lean point it grinds to. Many farms grind to a target, such as 80/20 or 85/15, and some offer more than one. Alderspring Ranch's listing describes its regular grind as roughly 80 to 85% lean and a fattier option at roughly 75 to 80%. ## Which lean/fat ratio to choose

Extension specialists give similar advice, though the exact numbers differ by source:

| Use | Suggested grind | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Crumbles you drain: tacos, chili, sloppy joes, pasta sauce | 73% lean or more (Arkansas); 90/10 or 95/5 (SDSU) | University of Arkansas, SDSU Extension |
| Meatloaf, meatballs, casseroles | 80% lean or more | University of Arkansas; SDSU says higher fat may suit these |
| Burgers and patties | Higher-fat grinds; Arkansas warns very lean beef makes a dry patty | SDSU Extension, University of Arkansas |
| Mixed dishes where you want less fat in the pan | 85% lean or more | University of Arkansas |

Texas A&M meat scientists note that higher-fat ground beef tends to be more tender, juicy and flavorful, especially when cooked to a higher doneness. Leaner grinds lose more moisture during cooking, while fattier grinds lose more fat. That matters when you compare price per pound: some of what you pay for in a 75/25 pack ends up in the pan.

Texas A&M describes modern ground beef as a blend of trimmings mixed to hit a set lean/fat target, so the ratio on the package reflects that blend, whatever the cattle ate. If you want to understand the feeding labels themselves, see [grass-fed vs grass-finished](/guides/grass-fed-vs-grass-finished/).

## Does buying a bundle save money?

Sometimes. Three things decide it:

1. **The bundle discount itself.** At US Wellness, the regular bundle price matched the single-pack price; the savings came from a sale. At Grass Roots, the 5 pound pack cost about 10% less per pound than single packs. At White Oak, the discount came only with a subscription.
2. **Shipping.** Each seller waives shipping above a threshold ($149 to $249, or 15 pounds at US Wellness). At Grass Roots, for example, an order under $149 pays $35 to ship, which more than doubles the cost of a single $13.95 pound. A bundle almost always clears the free-shipping line.
3. **Freezer space and storage time.** A 25 pound bundle of 1 pound packs fits in a small chest freezer, but it is a lot for a freezer-top compartment. The University of Arkansas extension sheet suggests using frozen raw ground beef within 3 to 4 months for best quality. SDSU Extension says vacuum-sealed packs and chubs freeze well, while foam trays with plastic overwrap do not.

If you eat a lot of ground beef and have room, buying a share of a whole animal from a local farm can be another route, since part of every share comes back as ground beef. ## A quick checklist before you order

- Look for the lean/fat ratio and decide which grind fits how you cook.
- Check the feeding wording. "Grass-fed and grass-finished" or "100% grass-fed" tells you more than "grass-fed" alone.
- Note any certification the seller names, and remember that many sellers name none.
- Work out the price per pound including shipping, at the order size you will actually buy.
- Make sure you have freezer space for a bundle before it arrives.
- Ask a small farm what lean point it grinds to if the label or website does not say.
