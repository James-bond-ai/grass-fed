The fastest way to tell whether a farm's grass-fed beef, lamb or dairy is what you expect is to ask the farmer a few direct questions before you pay a deposit. Good farms answer easily and specifically. The twelve questions below cover diet, drugs, labels, processing, pricing and logistics, with what a solid answer sounds like for each.

Find farms near you in our [farm directory](/farms/), then use this list by phone, email or at the market.

## Why you need to ask at all

"Grass-fed" has no single legal definition you can rely on at a farm stand. USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service used to publish a grass-fed standard, but it withdrew that standard in January 2016, saying defining label claims was outside its legal mandate. Since then, a grass-fed claim on a package is reviewed by the Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS, the USDA agency that inspects meat), which checks the producer's paperwork for that label. Meat sold straight from a farm, especially as a share of a live animal, may never go through that label review at all.

That leaves you with two tools: third-party certification and good questions. If a farm is not certified, questions do most of the work.

## Diet and finishing

### 1. What do the animals eat from weaning until harvest?

**Why it matters.** "Finishing" is the last few months before slaughter, when an animal puts on most of its fat. Many cattle graze for most of their lives and then go to a feedlot for a grain diet. That beef can honestly say the animal ate grass, but it is not grass-finished. The old USDA standard (2007) required grass and forage for the animal's whole life, with only mother's milk allowed before weaning. The American Grassfed Association (AGA), a certifier, uses similar wording: grass and forage only, from weaning until harvest.

**A good answer sounds like:** "They are on grass and forage their whole lives, including the last months. They are finished on pasture, not in a feedlot."

### 2. Do you ever feed grain, even a little?

**Why it matters.** Some farms give a bucket of grain to calm cattle, train them to come to a call, or add weight before slaughter. Others use "creep feed," grain placed where only calves can reach it. The 2007 standard said animals "cannot be fed grain or grain byproducts." Cereal crops grazed or cut before they form seed heads count as forage, which is why a farmer may say "we graze oats" without that being grain feeding.

**A good answer sounds like:** "No grain at any point. We do graze cereal crops in spring before they head out." Vague answers such as "just a little to finish them" mean the product is grain-finished.

### 3. What do they eat in winter or during a drought?

**Why it matters.** In most of the country, grass stops growing for part of the year. Animals then eat stored forage: hay (dried grass), haylage or baleage (grass wrapped and fermented while moist), or silage. The 2007 standard listed these as acceptable, and AGA allows them in bad weather or when pasture quality is low. Corn silage that contains the grain, though, is a grain source.

**A good answer sounds like:** "We feed our own hay and baleage from November to April. If we run short, we buy hay, not grain." Ask whether any silage is corn silage with the ears included.

### 4. What supplements do you use?

**Why it matters.** Mineral and salt blocks are normal and good husbandry. The 2007 standard allowed routine mineral and vitamin supplements. The problem is protein or energy tubs and pellets that are made from grain or grain byproducts (such as distillers grains or corn gluten).

**A good answer sounds like:** "Free-choice minerals and salt. No protein tubs or pellets." If they do use a tub or pellet, ask to see the ingredient label.

### 5. How much of the year are they actually on pasture?

**Why it matters.** An animal can be fed hay in a dirt pen all year and technically eat only forage. Pasture access is a separate question. The 2007 standard required "continuous access to pasture during the growing season." AGA prohibits confinement except for emergencies or short tasks like sorting and shipping. For comparison, USDA organic rules require at least 120 days of grazing a year, but they also let organic slaughter cattle be finished on grain in a yard or feedlot for up to 120 days, which is why "organic" is not the same as "grass-finished."

**A good answer sounds like:** "They graze from about April to November and move to fresh pasture every few days. In winter they are on a sacrifice paddock or hay field with free access to shelter." Rotational grazing, where animals move between paddocks on a schedule, is a sign of a farmer who manages grass closely.

## Drugs and labels

### 6. Do you use antibiotics? What happens to an animal you treat?

**Why it matters.** A responsible farmer treats a sick animal. What you want to know is whether that animal stays in the grass-fed meat they sell to you. AGA requires treated animals to be tagged and removed from the certified program, with written medication records. In 2024 FSIS said a study of beef cattle from the "Raised Without Antibiotics" market found antibiotic residues in about 20% of samples, and it now encourages testing or third-party certification to back up those claims.

**A good answer sounds like:** "We treat sick animals and record it. Treated animals are sold separately, not as our grass-fed beef." Be wary of "we never have sick animals."

### 7. Do you use growth hormone implants?

**Why it matters.** The FDA has approved steroid hormone implants (small pellets placed under the skin of the ear) to speed growth in beef cattle and sheep. They are not approved for growth in pigs or poultry, which is why a "no hormones" claim on pork or chicken must, under FSIS rules, carry the statement that federal regulations prohibit hormone use. So the hormone question matters for beef and lamb, not for pork or chicken. AGA prohibits hormones of any type.

**A good answer sounds like:** "No implants, ever." For beef from a sale barn calf, ask whether the farmer knows the calf's history before they bought it.

### 8. Are you certified, and by whom?

**Why it matters.** Third-party certification means an outside group audits the farm on site against a written standard. AGA, for example, inspects certified producers at least every 15 months. FSIS strongly encourages third-party certification for animal-raising claims, and under its 2024 guideline a certified claim on a label must name the certifier and its website.

**A good answer sounds like:** "Yes, we are certified by [named program]. Here is our listing on their website." Check the listing yourself. Learn what each label requires on our [certifications page](/certifications/). If the farm is not certified, that alone is not a red flag, but the answers to questions 1 through 7 then carry more weight.

## Processing and pricing

### 9. Where are the animals processed, and under what kind of inspection?

**Why it matters.** This decides what the farmer is legally allowed to sell you and how. There are three common setups:

| Processing type | Who inspects | How you can buy it |
|---|---|---|
| USDA-inspected plant | FSIS inspector on site | Individual cuts, by the package, across state lines |
| State-inspected plant | State program rated at least equal to federal | Individual cuts, but sold only within that state |
| Custom-exempt plant | No inspector at slaughter | Only as a share of a live animal you already own |

Under federal rules (9 CFR 303.1), custom-exempt meat is only for the owner's household, nonpaying guests and employees, and it must be marked "Not for Sale." That is why custom-exempt beef is sold as a whole, half or quarter "share": you buy part of the live animal, then the processor cuts it for you. The farmer cannot legally sell you individual steaks from a custom-exempt animal. State rules vary, so check with your state agriculture department.

**A good answer sounds like:** "Steaks and ground beef by the pound are processed at [named] USDA-inspected plant. Halves and quarters go to [named] custom-exempt butcher, and you pay them directly for cutting." A farmer selling individual packages stamped "Not for Sale" is a red flag.

### 10. How do you price it, and on what weight?

**Why it matters.** Bulk beef prices are quoted on very different weights, and a low price per pound can cost more in the end:

- **Live weight** is the whole animal on the hoof.
- **Hanging weight** (also called carcass weight) is what is left after the head, hide, organs and blood are removed. For beef, it is typically about 60% to 63% of live weight, according to SDSU Extension (2022), and UW-Madison Extension notes grass finishing tends to lower that percentage.
- **Take-home weight** is the packaged meat you actually get. UW-Madison Extension puts it at about 71% of the chilled carcass for bone-in cuts and 62% for boneless cuts.

So a half with a 600-pound chilled hanging weight can come home as roughly 370 to 430 pounds of meat, depending on how you have it cut. Also ask whether the price includes processing. Often you pay the farmer for the animal and the processor separately for slaughter, cutting and wrapping.

**A good answer sounds like:** "It is $X per pound hanging weight, paid to us, plus the butcher's cut-and-wrap fee, about $Y per pound. A deposit holds your spot." Prices vary widely by region and year, so get the exact numbers from the farm in writing.

### 11. How do pickup and delivery work?

**Why it matters.** Shares are often booked months ahead. University of Missouri Extension reports processor waits of 6 to 12 months in many areas. You also need freezer room: SDSU Extension suggests about one cubic foot of freezer space for every 35 to 40 pounds of packaged meat.

Ask:

- When will the animal be processed, and when can I pick up?
- Do I fill out a cut sheet (a form telling the butcher how thick to cut steaks, how much to grind, and package sizes)?
- Do I pick up at the farm, the butcher, or a drop point? Is delivery frozen, and what does it cost?
- What happens to my deposit if processing is delayed?

**A good answer sounds like:** a specific month, a named butcher, and someone who will walk you through the cut sheet.

### 12. Can I visit the farm?

**Why it matters.** A visit lets you see the pasture, the herd and the winter feeding setup for yourself. Expect to arrange a time in advance, since animals and farm work come first. University of Missouri Extension also suggests asking a farm for references from past customers.

**A good answer sounds like:** "Sure, we do farm tours on Saturdays, just let us know you are coming." A firm "no visitors, ever" with no reason, combined with vague feeding answers, is worth weighing.

## Putting it together

No farm has to answer all twelve perfectly. A small family farm may not be certified and may process at a custom-exempt butcher, and still raise animals exactly the way you want. What you are looking for is specific, consistent answers: a named processor, a clear winter feeding plan, a written price on a stated weight, and a farmer who is happy to explain.

Start by browsing [farms in your state](/farms/), shortlist two or three, and send each the same questions so you can compare answers side by side. If a label comes up that you don't recognize, look it up on our [certifications page](/certifications/).
