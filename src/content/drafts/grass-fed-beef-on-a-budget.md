The biggest savings on grass-fed beef come from three habits: buying the less popular cuts, buying a share of an animal (and splitting it if it is too much), and making sure nothing you pay for ends up thrown away. None of these needs special skill. They mostly need a plan, a slow cooker or heavy pot, and some freezer space.

This guide covers the practical side. If you want to know what grass-fed beef costs next to grocery beef, and what the extra money buys, start with our guide on [whether grass-fed beef is worth the price](/guides/is-grass-fed-beef-worth-it/).

## Step 1: buy the cuts other shoppers skip

Steaks from the rib and loin (ribeye, strip, tenderloin) cost the most because they are tender and quick to cook. Cuts from the muscles a cow uses more, such as the shoulder (chuck) and the back leg (round), cost less. A University of Arkansas extension specialist, writing in August 2026, said budget cuts usually come from the chuck, flank, sirloin and round.

The price gap can be large even between neighboring cuts. In the same Arkansas article, extension instructor Janeal Yancey pointed out that the chuck eye steak sits right next to the ribeye, one rib apart. She put typical store prices at about $14 a pound for chuck eye and $20 a pound for ribeye. Those figures were for regular grocery beef in Arkansas in 2026, not grass-fed, but the same pattern holds on most farms' price lists: ask for the cut next door to the expensive one.

Here are budget cuts that extension services recommend, and how to cook them.

| Cut | Where it comes from | How to cook it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chuck eye steak | Shoulder, next to the ribeye | Like a ribeye: grill or pan-sear | University of Arkansas |
| Top blade (flat iron) steak | Shoulder | Grill, roast or broil | University of Minnesota |
| Chuck arm roast | Shoulder | Slow-cook, pressure cook or braise | University of Minnesota |
| Sirloin cap (picanha, culotte) | Sirloin | Grill; eats much like a strip steak | University of Arkansas |
| Sirloin flap (bavette) | Bottom sirloin | Grill, skillet, stir-fry or broil | University of Minnesota |
| Sirloin tip | Round, near the sirloin | Kabobs, stew meat, cubed steak | University of Minnesota |
| Eye of round, top and bottom round | Back leg | Roasts: slow oven, braise or sous vide. Steaks: braise or stir-fry | University of Minnesota |
| Skirt steak (inside is cheaper than outside) | Plate, below the ribs | Trim the membrane; grill or sear hot and fast | University of Minnesota |

Minnesota Extension calls eye of round a "tremendous value" and suggests asking the butcher to remove tough membranes and extra fat before you buy. With thin cuts like skirt and flap, let the meat rest and slice it against the grain (across the visible muscle fibers), which makes each bite easier to chew.

When you shop at a farm stand or online farm store, look for these names on the price list and compare them with the steak prices. Our [farm directory](/farms/) lists farms by state so you can compare price lists near you.

## Step 2: cook the cheaper cuts so they are worth eating

A cheap cut cooked the wrong way can end up tough, and that feels like wasted money. The fix is usually low heat, liquid and time.

- **Braise or stew.** Montana State University Extension describes browning the meat, adding half a cup to 2 cups of liquid, then simmering on low on the stove or in a 325°F oven for one to three hours. Arkansas Extension's culinary specialist explains that long, moist cooking breaks down collagen (the tough connective tissue) into gelatin. A slow cooker, Dutch oven or electric pressure cooker all work.
- **Marinate thin steaks.** Minnesota Extension suggests 2 to 24 hours in the refrigerator, using a quarter to half a cup of marinade per pound of steak. Never reuse marinade that touched raw meat unless you boil it first.
- **Turn one roast into several meals.** Arkansas Extension suggests pot roast, shredded beef tacos and barbecue sandwiches from the same braised cut.

Grass-fed beef cooks a little differently from grocery beef. Our [worth-it guide](/guides/is-grass-fed-beef-worth-it/) covers why, with cooking tips from a grass-fed certifier.

## Step 3: make each pound go further

Ground beef is the most flexible budget item, and a few tricks stretch it.

- **Add fillers to meatloaf and meatballs.** Yancey suggests fiber, crackers or breadcrumbs, and she has added berries to beef to help hold moisture.
- **Mix it with other ingredients.** Beans, lentils, rice and chopped vegetables bulk up chili, tacos and pasta sauce, so a pound of grass-fed beef feeds more people.
- **Watch portion size.** Arkansas Extension describes a serving of beef as about 3 to 4 ounces cooked. If you are used to half-pound portions, smaller servings alongside other foods can halve what you buy.

## Step 4: buy a share, and split it if you need to

Buying a quarter, half or whole animal from a farm usually gives you a lower price per pound across all the cuts, steaks included. Yancey notes that a bulk purchase can mean you "might not have to buy beef again" for six months to a year, and she suggests splitting with another family if the amount is too much.

Splitting works best when you agree up front on three things:

1. **Who fills in the cut sheet** (the butcher's order form for how the beef is cut and packed). Ask the farm whether each household can submit its own. If not, agree together on steak thickness and how much goes to ground beef.
2. **How you will divide the packages.** Lay them out by type and take turns choosing.
3. **Who pays the deposit and the processing fee, and when.** Write it down.

Our guides on [buying a quarter cow](/guides/how-to-buy-a-quarter-cow/) and [choosing a quarter, half or whole](/guides/quarter-vs-half-vs-whole-cow/) explain hanging weight, take-home weight and fees. You can find farms that sell shares on our [bulk beef pages](/bulk-beef/).

Use the cut sheet to save money too. If your household mostly eats ground beef, ask the butcher to grind more of the less popular cuts. If you like slow-cooked meals, ask for more roasts and stew meat. Ask whether soup bones and organ meats are included with your share and what, if anything, they cost.

## Step 5: count the freezer, and don't let meat go stale

Bulk beef only saves money if you have somewhere to keep it and you eat it in time.

**Freezer running costs are modest.** ENERGY STAR estimates that one of its certified chest freezers uses about 215 kilowatt-hours a year, roughly $30 of electricity, compared with about 395 kilowatt-hours and $60 for a certified upright model. The Department of Energy says chest freezers are more efficient because they usually have more insulation and cold air doesn't spill out when you open the lid. Both agencies advise against buying a freezer bigger than you need, since larger models use more energy. ENERGY STAR adds that manual-defrost models use about half the energy of automatic-defrost ones, as long as you keep frost under a quarter of an inch.

**Set it to 0°F.** ENERGY STAR says to keep a freezer at 0°F. The Department of Energy suggests -5°F to 0°F: colder wastes electricity, and warmer shortens how long food keeps. A cheap appliance thermometer lets you check.

**Eat the ground beef first.** Montana State University Extension lists three to four months in the freezer for ground beef and six to twelve months for steaks and roasts, for best quality. A share often contains a lot of ground beef, so plan meals around it in the first few months. Freezer-burned meat that gets thrown out erases the savings from buying in bulk.

## Step 6: check what you are paying for on the label

At the grocery store, make sure a cheaper package is actually the product you want.

- **Grass-fed is not always grass-finished.** Some "grass-fed" beef was finished on grain. If that matters to you, see our guide to [grass-fed vs grass-finished](/guides/grass-fed-vs-grass-finished/) and the [certifications page](/certifications/).
- **"Product of USA" now has a stricter meaning.** Under a USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service rule that took effect January 1, 2026, meat labeled "Product of USA" or "Made in the USA" must come from animals born, raised, slaughtered and processed in the United States. Before that, according to Farm Policy News, imported meat could carry the claim if it passed through a USDA-inspected plant. The claim is voluntary, so a package without it is not necessarily imported. If origin matters to you, look for the claim or ask the seller.

When you buy straight from a farm, you can ask where the cattle were born and finished. Our list of [questions to ask a farm](/guides/how-to-vet-a-grass-fed-farm/) covers this.

## Step 7: ask whether the farm takes SNAP

If you receive SNAP (food stamp) benefits, USDA's Food and Nutrition Service lists meat among the foods SNAP can buy. Farmers and farmers markets can apply to accept SNAP, so some farm stands and market booths take EBT cards. The same USDA page says SNAP cannot buy live animals, except animals slaughtered before pick-up. USDA's page does not address bulk shares directly, so ask the farm whether it is SNAP-authorized and which of its products you can buy with benefits.

## A quick budget checklist

- Swap one steak dinner a week for a braised chuck or round roast.
- Ask for the chuck eye, flat iron or sirloin cap instead of the ribeye or strip.
- Stretch ground beef with beans, grains or breadcrumbs, and keep portions to 3 to 4 ounces cooked.
- Buy a quarter or half if you have space, or split one with friends or family.
- Shape the cut sheet around what you actually cook.
- Keep the freezer at 0°F and eat the ground beef within about four months.
- Read the label so you pay grass-fed prices only for the product you want.

Prices for both cuts and shares vary by farm, region and season. Always confirm current prices, what is included and how the cattle were raised with the farm before you order.
