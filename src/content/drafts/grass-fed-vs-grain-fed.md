Almost all US beef cattle start life eating grass. The real difference between "grass-fed" and "grain-fed" beef is what happens in the last several months: grain-fed cattle go to a feedlot and eat a grain-heavy ration, while grass-finished cattle keep eating grass and forage until harvest. That one stage changes how old the animal is at harvest, how much fat and marbling the meat has, how it cooks, and what it costs.

This guide is a starting point. It explains how each system works and points you to our more detailed guides on labels, nutrition and price.

## Two terms you need first

**Forage** means the leafy parts of plants that cattle can digest: pasture grass, legumes such as clover, and stored versions like hay and silage (fermented chopped plants).

**Finishing** is the last stage of raising beef cattle, when they put on weight and fat before harvest. Oklahoma State University (OSU) Extension describes it as the phase that takes a calf to slaughter weight.

Keep "finishing" in mind, because it is where the two systems part ways.

## How most US beef cattle are raised

The USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) describes the usual path for conventional, grain-finished beef in its cattle sector overview (updated May 2025):

1. **Cow-calf.** Calves are mostly born in spring and stay with their mothers on pasture until weaning at 3 to 7 months old. ERS notes that the cows graze forage with very little grain, if any.
2. **Stocker or backgrounding.** After weaning, some calves graze grass for another 3 to 4 months. Others spend 90 to 120 days on a mix of dry forage, silage and grain.
3. **Feedlot.** Cattle then move to a feedlot, a fenced yard where they are fed rather than grazing. ERS says they stay 90 to 300 days, depending on their age and weight when they arrive, their genetics, the weather, the ration and the quality grade the feeder is aiming for.

So "grain-fed" beef is not from an animal that ate grain its whole life. It usually grazed for months first, then was grain-finished.

## What grain-fed cattle eat in the feedlot

ERS says feedlot rations are generally 70 to 90 percent grain and protein concentrates, with the rest made up of roughage such as hay or silage. OSU Extension gives a slightly different picture for a finishing diet: about 50 to 60 percent corn, with byproducts such as distillers grains, corn gluten feed and soybean hulls often replacing some of the grain. The two figures measure slightly different things (grain plus protein concentrates versus corn alone), so read them as a range rather than a contradiction.

OSU Extension also notes that most cattle in feedlots receive growth-promoting implants. It cites a 2013 USDA survey finding that about 90 percent of cattle placed in feedlots were implanted at least once. Implants are a separate question from grass versus grain, so ask the farm if it matters to you.

## What grass-finished cattle eat

Grass-finished cattle skip the feedlot. They keep grazing pasture, and in winter or drought they eat stored forage such as hay or haylage. They are not fed grain.

These cattle gain weight more slowly. OSU Extension says calves finished on pasture tend to have lower gains, less fat and marbling, and a lower dressing percentage (the share of the live animal that ends up as carcass) than cattle finished in a dry lot. In practice that means they are usually older at harvest. In the Michigan State study described below, the grass-finished steers were harvested at 26 months and the feedlot steers at 18 months.

## Side by side

| | Grain-finished (conventional) | Grass-finished |
|---|---|---|
| Early life | Pasture with mother, often more grazing after weaning | Pasture with mother, then more pasture |
| Final months | Feedlot for 90 to 300 days (ERS) | Pasture, plus hay or other stored forage when grass is short |
| Final diet | Mostly grain and protein concentrates, with some roughage | Grass and forage only, no grain |
| Typical age at harvest | Younger (18 months in the Michigan study) | Older (26 months in the Michigan study) |
| Marbling | Higher | Lower (OSU Extension) |
| Where you usually buy it | Most grocery store beef | Farms, butcher shops, some grocery brands |

## The label problem: "grass-fed" does not always mean grass-finished

This is the part that trips up most shoppers. Because nearly all cattle eat grass early in life, a label that says only "grass-fed" may not tell you how the animal was finished. Some products sold as grass-fed come from cattle that were grain-finished.

If you want beef from cattle that ate no grain, look for "grass-finished" or "100% grass-fed," ideally backed by a third-party certification. Our guide to [grass-fed vs grass-finished](/guides/grass-fed-vs-grass-finished/) walks through USDA's labeling guidance in detail, and the [certifications page](/certifications/) explains what each program checks, including which ones allow grain in some situations.

In our [farm directory](/farms/), every listing shows how the farm itself describes its feeding, with a link to the page where it says so. Some farms sell both kinds: grass-finished beef and beef that grazed and then got grain before harvest. If a listing says finishing is not stated, ask the farm before you order.

## How the beef eats and cooks

Less marbling (the thin streaks of fat inside the muscle) changes the eating experience. The clearest US comparison we found is a two-year Michigan State University study by Maciel and colleagues, published in *Translational Animal Science* in 2021. It followed 104 steers of two Angus-based types, finished either on diverse pasture or in a feedlot. Steaks were aged 14 days, then tested with a machine that measures how much force it takes to cut the meat, and served to a panel of 105 tasters who scored them on a 9-point scale.

The results:

- The grass-finished steaks took more force to cut, meaning they were less tender by that measure.
- Tasters scored the grain-finished steaks better on the sensory traits measured.
- The authors linked the grain-finished steaks' texture and juiciness to their higher marbling.
- The grass-finished meat was a different color, redder and darker than the grain-finished meat.

The authors point out limits worth knowing. The two groups came from different herds and locations, and the grass-finished steers were older at harvest, so the finishing system was not the only difference between them. One study of two cattle types in one state also cannot speak for every farm. Grass-finished beef varies a lot with pasture, breed, age and how the meat is aged.

What this means for you: grass-finished steaks are leaner and can turn tough if you cook them like a fatty grocery steak. Use a meat thermometer, take steaks off the heat a little earlier, and consider slow, moist cooking for roasts. Some people prefer the flavor of grass-finished beef and some do not. Before committing to a big order, buy a pound of ground beef and a steak from the farm you are considering.

## Nutrition: real differences, small amounts

Grass-finished beef tends to be leaner, and studies have measured more omega-3 fat in it than in grain-finished beef. But beef holds little omega-3 either way, and the amounts are small compared with fish such as salmon. Results also vary a lot from farm to farm, and some grain-finished samples match grass-finished ones.

We cover the measured numbers, with absolute amounts per serving and a salmon comparison, in our guide on [whether grass-fed beef is worth the price](/guides/is-grass-fed-beef-worth-it/). If nutrition is your main reason for paying more, read that first.

## Price: why grass-finished costs more

Grass-finished cattle grow more slowly, are older at harvest and carry less fat, so each animal takes longer to raise and yields less beef. Many grass-finished producers are also smaller farms that pay for small-scale processing and delivery. Together, those explain much of the price gap you see between farm-direct grass-finished beef and grocery beef.

Our [worth-it guide](/guides/is-grass-fed-beef-worth-it/) compares 2026 USDA farm-direct grass-fed prices with national grocery averages. The cheapest way to buy grass-finished beef is usually a share of an animal: see [how to buy a quarter cow](/guides/how-to-buy-a-quarter-cow/), [which share size fits your household](/guides/quarter-vs-half-vs-whole-cow/) and our [bulk beef pages by state](/bulk-beef/). Prices vary by region and year, so always confirm with the farm.

## Land and climate: no simple winner

You will see strong claims in both directions. Grain finishing produces more beef per animal in less time, while grass finishing keeps cattle on pasture and uses forage people cannot eat. Published studies weigh these trade-offs differently and do not agree on which system comes out ahead overall. If land and grazing practices matter to you, ask the farm what it actually does, for example how often it moves cattle between paddocks (sections of pasture), rather than relying on the label. Our [worth-it guide](/guides/is-grass-fed-beef-worth-it/) summarizes the research we have reviewed.

## Questions to ask a farm

If you are choosing between grass-finished and grain-finished beef from a local farm, these questions get you a clear answer:

- Do your cattle eat any grain at any stage? If so, when and how much?
- What do they eat in winter or during a drought?
- How old are they at harvest?
- Do you use growth implants?
- Is your grass-fed claim certified by anyone? Which program?
- How long do you dry-age or wet-age the beef?

Our [guide to vetting a grass-fed farm](/guides/how-to-vet-a-grass-fed-farm/) has more questions and what good answers sound like.

## Which should you buy?

Choose grass-finished beef if you want to know your beef came from cattle that ate only grass and forage, and you are willing to pay more and cook a little more carefully. Choose grain-finished beef if you prefer heavy marbling and the lowest price per pound. Either way, read past the word "grass-fed" on the label, look for "grass-finished" or a certification, and confirm the details with the farm. You can [find farms near you](/farms/) and see exactly how each one describes its feeding.
