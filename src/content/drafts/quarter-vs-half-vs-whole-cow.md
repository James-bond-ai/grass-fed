Pick the share your household can eat within about a year, and whose ground beef you can finish in three or four months. By our estimate, a quarter of a grass-finished steer gives a small household (one to three people) a bit over 2 pounds of beef a week for a year, a half suits a family that eats beef most days, and a whole animal usually makes sense only if you split it with other households.

## Share sizes at a glance

Farms sell beef by the share: a fraction of one animal's carcass, cut and wrapped by a butcher. The table below is our own estimate for a grass-finished animal. It starts from one Oklahoma State University fact sheet (ANSI-3292) that lists a grass-finished carcass at about 638 pounds hanging weight (the weight after slaughter, before cutting), compared with about 832 pounds for a grain-finished one. Oklahoma State's *Buying Beef in Large Quantities* says cutting losses run 20 to 40 percent and are normally 25 to 30 percent, so we used 70 to 75 percent of hanging weight as the meat you take home.

| Share | Hanging weight (estimate) | Take-home meat (estimate) | Beef per week over 12 months | Freezer space at 35 to 40 lb per cu ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eighth | about 80 lb | 56 to 60 lb | about 1.1 lb | 1.4 to 1.7 cu ft |
| Quarter | about 160 lb | 112 to 120 lb | about 2.2 lb | 2.8 to 3.4 cu ft |
| Half | about 320 lb | 223 to 239 lb | about 4.4 lb | 5.6 to 6.8 cu ft |
| Whole | about 640 lb | 447 to 478 lb | about 8.9 lb | 11.2 to 13.7 cu ft |

Your animal may differ: larger cattle yield more, and boneless cuts or very lean ground beef lower your take-home weight. Ask the farm for the expected hanging weight of the animals it is harvesting this season. Our [quarter cow guide](/guides/how-to-buy-a-quarter-cow/) explains hanging weight, take-home weight and processing fees in detail.

## Step 1: work out how much beef you eat

Start with your own habits. For two to four weeks, write down the beef you buy and cook at home, in pounds. Multiply your weekly figure by 52 and compare it with the take-home column above.

If you need a rough benchmark, the USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) estimated beef disappearance (supply minus exports and stocks, divided by population) at 59.7 retail pounds per person for 2024. That counts all beef, including restaurant meals and food that is never eaten, so it overstates what most people cook at home. By that measure alone, a quarter would be about two people's beef for a year, a half about four people's and a whole about eight. Treat that only as an upper bound.

## Step 2: check how fast you will need to eat it

The share that fits is often decided by the ground beef, not the total. Ground beef is a big part of every share:

- NC State Extension's example quarter includes 40 to 50 pounds of ground beef, 30 to 40 pounds of steaks, about 10 pounds of stew meat and a 6 pound roast.
- University of Minnesota Extension's example quarter, from a 1,300 pound animal yielding about 142 pounds, includes 35 to 60 pounds of ground beef plus 7 ribeyes, 6 T-bones, 3 sirloin steaks, 3 sirloin tip steaks, 3 round steaks and 6 roasts.

Now look at storage times. FoodSafety.gov lists 4 to 12 months in the freezer for beef steaks and roasts and 3 to 4 months for ground meat. These times are for quality: the chart notes that food kept frozen at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, but taste and texture decline. Oklahoma State Extension (AFS-3401) gives 9 to 12 months for beef and 3 to 4 months for ground beef, and warns that longer storage brings more freezer burn and broken packages.

The math that catches people out: if a quarter holds 40 to 50 pounds of ground beef and you want to use it within four months, that is about 2.5 to 3 pounds of ground beef a week, on top of the steaks and roasts. A half doubles that. If your household can't keep up, you have three options:

- Choose a smaller share.
- Ask the butcher to turn less of the carcass into ground beef and more into roasts and stew meat, which keep longer.
- Plan to eat the ground beef first and the steaks and roasts later.

Oklahoma State also suggests the reverse for heavy ground beef users: have cuts your family won't eat ground, or consider a forequarter, which has more of the cuts that usually go to ground beef and pot roast.

## Step 3: measure your freezer

Oklahoma State Extension says to allow 1 cubic foot of freezer space for every 35 to 40 pounds of cut and wrapped beef, and more for oddly shaped packages. Minnesota Extension is more generous: it recommends at least 4.5 cubic feet in a chest freezer, or 5.5 cubic feet in an upright, for a 142 pound quarter.

A few practical rules follow from that:

- **The freezer above your fridge is usually not enough for a quarter.** NC State Extension says an average home refrigerator freezer holds about an eighth of a beef, roughly 50 to 60 pounds, and that a stand-alone freezer holds meat colder.
- **A quarter fits a small chest freezer** of around 5 cubic feet, with room for other food if the animal is on the small side.
- **A half needs a medium chest freezer** of roughly 7 to 9 cubic feet, by our reading of the two extension rules above.
- **A whole animal needs a large freezer or two**, at 12 to 16 cubic feet or more.

If you are buying a freezer for this, Oklahoma State lists the freezer itself and the cost of running it among the expenses to include when you compare bulk beef with store prices.

## Step 4: compare price per pound and upfront cost

Bigger shares usually cost less per pound, but you pay more at once. The most recent national figures we found are in the USDA Agricultural Marketing Service (AMS) National Grass Fed Beef Report for July to September 2026, dated September 25, 2026. Its general direct-to-consumer carcass tables (not its grass-fed section, which had no share prices this quarter) listed these averages:

| Share | Avg price per lb hanging weight, processing not included | Avg price per lb of packaged meat |
|---|---|---|
| Eighth | not listed | $13.47 |
| Quarter | $6.23 | $13.30 |
| Half | $5.76 | $12.39 |
| Whole | $5.90 | $11.90 |

The same report lists processing at an average of $0.94 per pound of hanging weight and a slaughter fee averaging $125 per head. Because these are averages across whatever farms reported, the pattern is not perfectly tidy: this quarter, whole carcasses averaged slightly more per hanging pound than halves. The packaged-weight prices show the clearer trend, with each step up in size a little cheaper per pound. These are not grass-fed prices, and grass-finished farms may charge more or less. Prices vary by region and year, so confirm the current price with the farm.

Using those averages and our estimated grass-finished hanging weights, here is roughly what you would pay up front (beef plus processing plus your part of the slaughter fee):

- **Quarter (160 lb hanging):** about $1,180, or about $9.80 to $10.50 per pound of take-home meat
- **Half (320 lb hanging):** about $2,200, or about $9.20 to $9.90 per pound
- **Whole (640 lb hanging):** about $4,490, or about $9.40 to $10.10 per pound

In this example, the half and whole come out a little cheaper per pound than the quarter, but the difference is small next to the cost of beef you throw away because it sat too long. If the farm takes a deposit when you reserve, ask how much it is and when the balance is due, since the final bill depends on the hanging weight.

## Step 5: decide how much control you want over the cuts

The size of your share changes how much say you have over the cut sheet (the form that tells the butcher how to cut the beef).

- **Whole and half:** you usually get a full side (or two) to yourself, so you can fill in the cut sheet for all of it, choosing steak thickness, roast sizes, package sizes and how much goes to ground beef.
- **Quarter:** it depends on the farm. A traditional quarter is a **forequarter** (chuck, rib, brisket, plate) or a **hindquarter** (round, loin, flank). Oklahoma State says the hindquarter gives more steaks and roasts but costs more per pound, while the forequarter has more less-tender cuts and costs less per pound. Some farms instead sell a mixed or "split" quarter with cuts from both ends, where you share a side, and possibly its cut sheet, with another buyer. Ask which kind you are buying and whether you get your own instructions.
- **Eighth:** where farms offer one, ask whether you get any choice of cuts or a set bundle. It is a smaller-risk way to try a new farm's beef before committing to a bigger share.

Minnesota Extension puts the trade-off plainly: there are only so many steaks to go around on one animal, so check which cuts you will actually get before you commit.

## Splitting a half or whole with others

Buying a whole or half and dividing it among friends or family can get you the lower per-pound price without a freezer full of beef. Minnesota Extension describes splitting a whole animal among two or more households as economical. Before you commit, agree on:

1. **Who orders and pays.** One person usually places the order and pays the deposit. Agree how you will split the slaughter fee and processing.
2. **The cut sheet.** Decide whether you will use one set of instructions or each take your own. If the beef is processed at a custom-exempt shop (where it is cut for the owners of the animal, not for sale), Oregon State University Extension says the share owners, not the farm, call the butcher with cutting instructions, and if the owners agree, one of them can make the call for everyone.
3. **Odd items and pickup.** Agree who gets single items such as the brisket, tongue or oxtail, and have every freezer ready for the same pickup day.

With custom-exempt beef, each owner is buying part of a live animal. Oregon State Extension says all shares must be sold before slaughter, each owner's name is recorded by the processor, and packages are marked "not for sale." It also says that in Oregon, more than four shares in one animal may draw extra attention from regulators. Rules differ by state; ask your farm.

## Which share should you buy?

A starting point, to adjust for your freezer and cooking habits:

- **Trying grass-finished beef for the first time, or one or two people who eat beef once or twice a week:** an eighth, or a quarter split with someone.
- **Two to four people who eat beef a few times a week, with a small chest freezer:** a quarter.
- **A family that eats beef most days, with a medium chest freezer:** a half.
- **Large households, or several households splitting the cost:** a whole.

Whatever size you choose, ask how the animal was finished.
