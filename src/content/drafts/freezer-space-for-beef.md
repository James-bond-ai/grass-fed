A quarter cow usually needs about 3 to 5 cubic feet of freezer space, and a half needs about 6 to 11, once you allow for odd-shaped packages and the room shelves take up. The freezer on top of your refrigerator is not enough: it holds roughly an eighth of a beef. If you are buying a freezer for your share, a small chest freezer (about 5 cubic feet) fits most quarters, and a chest freezer of about 7 to 9 cubic feet fits most halves.

## The basic math: pounds per cubic foot

Freezers are sold by volume in cubic feet. Beef is sold by weight. To connect the two, extension services use a packing rule of thumb, and they don't all agree:

- **Oklahoma State University Extension:** allow 1 cubic foot for every 35 to 40 pounds of cut and wrapped beef, and more for oddly shaped packages.
- **University of Minnesota Extension:** for a quarter that yields about 142 pounds of meat, have at least 4.5 cubic feet in a chest freezer or 5.5 cubic feet in an upright. That works out to roughly 32 pounds per cubic foot in a chest and 26 in an upright, by our math.
- **NC State Extension (Henderson County):** 50 pounds of meat fits in about 2.25 cubic feet, or about 22 pounds per cubic foot.

The spread is wide, from about 22 to 40 pounds per cubic foot, because tidy flat packs stack tighter than roasts and bones. If you have to pick one, plan with the Minnesota figures and treat Oklahoma's rule as the tightest you can pack.

## How much space each share needs

Take-home weight (the cut, wrapped meat you actually store) depends on the animal and your cut sheet. NC State Extension estimates 100 to 140 pounds for a quarter. Oklahoma State says a side (a half) of an average 600 pound carcass yields about 225 pounds of usable beef, and a whole carcass about 450. Our guide on [how to buy a quarter cow](/guides/how-to-buy-a-quarter-cow/) explains how hanging weight turns into take-home weight, and [quarter vs half vs whole cow](/guides/quarter-vs-half-vs-whole-cow/) covers splitting a share.

| Share | Take-home beef (estimate) | Tight packing, 35 to 40 lb per cu ft | Chest freezer, about 32 lb per cu ft | Upright freezer, about 26 lb per cu ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eighth | 50 to 70 lb | 1.3 to 2 cu ft | 1.6 to 2.2 cu ft | 1.9 to 2.7 cu ft |
| Quarter | 100 to 140 lb | 2.5 to 4 cu ft | 3.2 to 4.4 cu ft | 3.9 to 5.4 cu ft |
| Half | 200 to 280 lb | 5 to 8 cu ft | 6.3 to 8.9 cu ft | 7.8 to 10.9 cu ft |
| Whole | 400 to 560 lb | 10 to 16 cu ft | 12.7 to 17.7 cu ft | 15.5 to 21.7 cu ft |

This table is our own estimate. The take-home ranges for the eighth, half and whole are scaled from NC State's quarter figure, and the chest and upright columns are scaled from Minnesota's 142 pound quarter. Bone-in roasts, soup bones and organ meats take more room per pound than flat packs of ground beef. Your butcher's packaging matters too, so ask how they wrap.

Then add whatever already lives in your freezer. If you keep frozen vegetables, bread and ice in the same box, measure what is free, not the size on the label.

## Why the label size is not the space you get

The capacity printed on a freezer is its total interior volume. Some of it is hard to use:

- **Shelves and bins.** Consumer Reports says the shelves and bins in upright freezers can take up as much as 20 percent of the space. That is why Minnesota Extension asks for a full cubic foot more in an upright than in a chest.
- **Odd shapes.** Oklahoma State says to allow more space when packages are oddly shaped. Bone-in rib roasts and shanks don't stack like flat packs.
- **Door shelves.** In Consumer Reports tests, door shelves on manual-defrost uprights ran 9°F to 19°F warmer than the rest of the freezer. Keep beef off the door.

## Will it fit in the freezer you already have?

Probably not, if your only freezer sits above or beside your refrigerator. NC State Extension says the freezer in an average home refrigerator usually holds about one eighth of a beef, roughly 50 to 60 pounds, and only when it is otherwise empty. Anything bigger needs a stand-alone freezer or a second refrigerator-freezer. NC State also notes that a stand-alone freezer can usually hold meat at a colder temperature.

Check what kind of freezer you have, too. Clemson Extension separates a true freezer, which holds 0°F or colder, from a freezing compartment (the small box inside some refrigerators), and says food in a freezing compartment keeps for only one to two weeks. A small compartment like that is not a place to store a share.

## Chest or upright?

Consumer Reports says neither type is a clear winner. Here is how they compare, based on the sources below:

| | Chest freezer | Upright freezer |
|---|---|---|
| Usable space | Wide open interior | Shelves and bins can use up to 20% (Consumer Reports) |
| Floor space | Larger footprint: a 22 cu ft chest is about 2 by 6 feet (Consumer Reports) | About 2.5 by 2.5 feet for the same size (Consumer Reports) |
| Energy use | About 215 kWh and $30 a year for an ENERGY STAR model (ENERGY STAR) | About 395 kWh and $60 a year for an ENERGY STAR model (ENERGY STAR) |
| Defrosting | Most need manual defrosting (Consumer Reports) | Manual and self-defrost models both sold |
| Finding things | Digging through layers; baskets help | Shelves make it easier to organize (ENERGY STAR) |
| Power outages | Kept food frozen longer in Consumer Reports tests | Some let temperatures rise significantly after about 9 hours (Consumer Reports) |
| Price range | About $170 to $1,200 (Consumer Reports, undated) | About $450 to $2,100 (Consumer Reports, undated) |

Virginia Cooperative Extension says a chest freezer generally uses 10 to 25 percent less energy than an upright, partly because cold air doesn't spill out when you open the lid. The U.S. Department of Energy says chest models are more efficient because they typically have more insulation.

Defrost type matters for energy too. ENERGY STAR says manual-defrost models use half the energy of automatic-defrost ones; Virginia Extension puts the saving at 35 to 40 percent. Either way, manual defrost uses less. Consumer Reports notes that self-defrost uprights tend to be noisier and may cost more to run.

Consumer Reports groups freezers into compact or small (5 to 9 cubic feet), medium (12 to 18) and large (more than 18). Check the EnergyGuide label and current prices before you buy.

### Don't buy more freezer than you need

Bigger freezers use more electricity. ENERGY STAR says to buy an appropriately sized freezer, and the Department of Energy says oversized freezers cost more, take more space and waste energy. If you will buy a quarter once a year, a small chest freezer is likely enough. If you plan on a half, or on a quarter plus a hog, size up.

Count the running cost when you compare bulk beef with store prices, as Oklahoma State Extension advises. As a rough example (our math): an ENERGY STAR chest freezer at about $30 a year, holding 120 pounds of beef, adds about 25 cents a pound per year of storage.

## Where to put it

- **Indoors if you can.** ENERGY STAR advises keeping a freezer indoors, such as in a basement, unless you live in a mild climate, because extreme temperatures are hard on the compressor.
- **Check the garage rating.** Consumer Reports says most manufacturers allow operation up to 110°F, and advises avoiding spots above 110°F or below 0°F. If it will go in a garage, read the product specifications first.
- **Leave breathing room.** ENERGY STAR says to leave a few inches of clearance behind it. Virginia Extension adds: keep the coils clean and check that the door seals close tightly.

## Get ready before pickup day

**Set the temperature.** ENERGY STAR and USDA FSIS (the Food Safety and Inspection Service) both say to keep frozen food at 0°F. The Department of Energy gives a range of -5°F to 0°F and suggests an inexpensive appliance thermometer to check it. Turn a new freezer on a day or two ahead so it is cold when the beef arrives.

**Ask the butcher to freeze it hard.** Home freezers are slow at freezing a large load. Clemson Extension says to add only as much unfrozen food as will freeze within 24 hours, usually 2 to 3 pounds per cubic foot of storage space. For a 5 cubic foot freezer, that is only 10 to 15 pounds a day. Oklahoma State says meat should be frozen at -10°F or lower as quickly as possible, and recommends commercial quick-freezing for large purchases. So ask your processor whether your beef will be frozen solid at pickup. Confirm before pickup day.

**Bring coolers.** Plan how you will keep 100 or more pounds of frozen beef cold on the drive home, and clear the freezer before you leave.

## Packing and organizing your share

- **Check the wrapping.** Clemson Extension says freezer packaging should resist moisture and vapor, and that torn or unsealed packages, or too much air inside, cause freezer burn (dry, light-colored patches). Set aside any torn packages and use them first.
- **Label and list.** Clemson recommends labeling each package with the product and date and keeping a dated inventory near the freezer. A list taped to the lid saves digging.
- **Put ground beef on top.** Ground beef keeps the shortest time. Oklahoma State gives 3 to 4 months for ground beef and 9 to 12 months for other beef. Clemson's chart gives 3 months for ground or stew meat and 12 months for steaks and roasts, and says to use the shorter times for best quality. A quarter often includes 35 to 60 pounds of ground beef (Minnesota Extension), so plan to cook through it first.
- **Keep it full.** Clemson notes that a full freezer is the most energy-efficient.
- **Defrost on schedule.** ENERGY STAR and Virginia Extension say not to let frost build up past a quarter inch. The easiest time to defrost a manual model is when your share is running low, before the next one arrives.

## If the power goes out

Keep the door closed. FoodSafety.gov says a full freezer holds a safe temperature for about 48 hours, and a half-full one about 24 hours, if the door stays shut. Clemson Extension says a fully loaded freezer can keep food frozen for two to four days, so sources differ, but both agree a full freezer lasts longer. Consumer Reports found that some uprights warmed significantly after about 9 hours, so don't count on the longest figure.

When power returns, FoodSafety.gov says frozen meat that still has ice crystals, or is at 40°F or below, can be refrozen, though quality may suffer. Meat held above 40°F for more than 2 hours should be thrown out. An appliance thermometer, which FSIS recommends keeping in the freezer, tells you which case you are in. Never taste food to decide whether it is safe.

## Quick checklist

1. Estimate your take-home weight with the farm and butcher.
2. Divide by about 32 pounds per cubic foot for a chest freezer, or 26 for an upright, and add room for what you already store.
3. If you need a freezer, consider a manual-defrost chest model, sized to the share you will buy most often.
4. Put it somewhere within the maker's temperature range, set it to 0°F, and add a thermometer.
5. Confirm your beef will be frozen solid at pickup, and bring coolers.
6. Label, list and use the ground beef first.
