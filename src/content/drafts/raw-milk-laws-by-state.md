Whether you can buy raw milk depends on the state you're in. A few states allow it on store shelves, many allow it only from the farm, some allow it only through a herdshare, and several allow no sale for people at all. Federal rules ban selling raw milk for drinking across state lines everywhere. The table below lists all 50 states, checked on October 26, 2026.

**This is a summary, not legal advice. Laws change; check with your state agriculture department.**

## What "raw milk" means here

Raw milk is milk that has not been pasteurized. Pasteurization means heating milk to a set temperature for a set time. Federal rules list several approved combinations, such as 161°F for 15 seconds or 145°F for 30 minutes ([21 CFR 1240.61](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-L/part-1240/subpart-D/section-1240.61)).

Raw milk is a separate question from grass-fed. A grass-fed dairy may or may not sell raw milk, and raw milk may come from cows fed mostly grain. If grazing matters to you, ask the farm about both. Our [certifications guide](/certifications/) explains the feeding labels.

## The five categories of state law

State laws vary in their details, but most fall into one of these groups. Many states fit more than one, so the table lists the most open legal route for human consumption.

**Retail sales allowed.** You can buy raw milk in a store, not only at the farm. These states usually require the dairy to hold a permit, pass regular testing and print a warning label.

**On-farm sales only.** You buy directly from the farmer, usually at the farm. Some of these states also allow delivery, farmers market sales or other direct sales that the farm handles itself. Some limit sales to small herds, small volumes or goat milk.

**Herdshare or cowshare.** No sale is allowed, but you can buy part ownership of a cow, goat or herd. You then pay the farmer to care for the animal and receive milk from it. More on how this works below.

**Pet food only.** Raw milk can be sold only as animal feed, labeled as not for human consumption. Louisiana's 2024 law, for example, requires a large printed warning to that effect on raw milk sold as pet food ([Louisiana Act 699](https://www.legis.la.gov/legis/ViewDocument.aspx?d=1382720)). Because pet milk is labeled not for human consumption, the table does not count it as a way to buy milk to drink. The Farm-to-Consumer Legal Defense Fund (FTCLDF) chart lists no legal sale for people to drink in these states, though sources disagree about Virginia herdshares (see the table).

**Prohibited.** No legal sale of any kind, and no herdshare law. On the FTCLDF chart, only Hawaii falls here. The National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) gives a different count: an estimated seven states and Washington, D.C. ban raw milk sales for people to drink, while at least 34 states allow them under conditions (NCSL, updated June 8, 2026). NCSL does not name the states behind either number or say how it treats pet-food-only states, so we can't line its count up with the table below. The two sources simply group states differently.

## How herdshares work

A herdshare (also called a cowshare or goat share) is a contract. You buy a share in an animal or a herd. You then pay the farmer a regular boarding fee to feed, house and milk the animal. The milk you collect is treated as coming from your own animal, so in theory no sale takes place.

North Carolina shows how a state can write this into law. A 2018 change to state law said that giving raw milk to a partial owner of a milking animal is not a "sale," according to NC State Extension. The same extension paper notes that the state agriculture department does not oversee or test milk from these agreements.

Not every state accepts the idea:

- **Some states allow herdshares by law or policy.** In Colorado, Michigan, North Carolina, Ohio and Tennessee, the FTCLDF chart lists no legal sale of raw milk for people but lists herdshares as legal, so a herdshare is the only route it shows.
- **Many states have no law either way.** FTCLDF marks many states as having no law that allows or bans herdshares. In those states the legal position is unsettled, and a regulator or court could still treat a herdshare as a sale.
- **Some states reject them.** Wisconsin's agriculture department says a herdshare does not count as real ownership of the cow. FTCLDF lists herdshares as barred by regulation in Maryland and by rule in Georgia. In Montana, FTCLDF says a traditional herdshare isn't allowed because the farmer must meet state requirements instead.
- **Courts have disagreed.** NC State Extension describes a Virginia court that treated one arrangement as a sham and a Maryland court that found another was enough like a sale to regulate.

Before you sign, read the contract closely. Ask who owns the animal if it dies or goes dry, what the boarding fee covers, how often the milk is tested and by whom, and what the contract says about liability. A contract may waive some of your rights, so consider having a lawyer review it.

## The federal ban on interstate sales

Federal rules say milk in final packaged form for drinking cannot be shipped, sold or distributed across state lines unless it has been pasteurized ([21 CFR 1240.61](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-L/part-1240/subpart-D/section-1240.61)). The rule allows some exceptions, such as certain aged cheeses.

This applies in every state, even states that allow retail sales. A Pennsylvania dairy can sell raw milk to Pennsylvania buyers, but it cannot legally ship that milk to a buyer in New Jersey.

## What the FDA and CDC say about safety

Both federal health agencies advise against drinking raw milk.

- The **FDA** ([The dangers of raw milk](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/dangers-raw-milk-unpasteurized-milk-can-pose-serious-health-risk)) says raw milk can carry germs such as *Salmonella*, *E. coli* and *Listeria*. It advises buying only pasteurized milk and dairy products and checking labels for the word "pasteurized."
- The **CDC** ([Raw milk](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/raw-milk.html)) lists *Campylobacter*, *Cryptosporidium*, *E. coli*, *Listeria*, *Brucella* and *Salmonella* as germs raw milk can carry. It says young children, older adults, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems face a higher chance of serious illness. It recommends choosing pasteurized milk.

NCSL also notes that the CDC reports rising outbreaks as raw milk access grows. Many state legislatures have chosen to allow some raw milk sales despite the federal advice. This site does not make health claims about raw milk either way. For questions about your own health, talk to your doctor.

## How to find raw milk legally where you live

1. **Find your state in the table**, then confirm with your state agriculture department. Rules can change in any legislative session; NCSL counted more than 40 raw milk bills introduced in 2026.
2. **Buy from inside your state.** Federal rules bar interstate sales of raw milk for drinking.
3. **Ask for the permit or registration** in states that require one, and ask how often the milk is tested.
4. **Read any herdshare contract** in full before you pay, and get your own legal advice if the terms are unclear.
5. **Search for a farm.** Our [farm directory](/farms/) lists dairies by state. A listing describes how a farm says it raises its animals; it does not mean the farm sells raw milk or that any sale is legal in your state.
