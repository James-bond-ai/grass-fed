Grass-fed ground beef bought from farms cost about 60 percent more per pound than grocery-store ground beef in 2026, based on national USDA and Bureau of Labor Statistics averages. Whether that is worth it depends on what you are paying for. If you want beef from cattle that ate grass and forage, from a farm you can name, the premium buys exactly that. If you are paying mainly for nutrition, the measured differences are real but small, and you can cut the price a lot by buying a share of an animal.

## What grass-fed beef costs in 2026

USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service (AMS) surveys farms that sell grass-fed beef straight to customers. Its third-quarter 2026 report, dated September 25, 2026, drew on 84 producers plus state reports from North Carolina and South Carolina. For grocery prices, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes a monthly national average. The two surveys measure different things (farm direct sales versus supermarket shelves), so treat the comparison as a rough guide.

| Product | Grass-fed, sold direct by farms (AMS, Q3 2026 average) | Grocery store, all beef (BLS, September 2026 average) |
|---|---|---|
| Ground beef, regular | $10.78 a pound (80 to 89% lean) | $6.79 a pound (100% ground beef) |
| Ground beef, lean | $13.76 a pound (90% lean or more) | $8.46 a pound (lean and extra lean) |
| Ribeye steak | $28.89 a pound | Not tracked by BLS |
| Strip steak | $26.42 a pound | Not tracked by BLS |
| Share of an animal, price per pound of meat you take home | $11.90 (whole) to $13.47 (eighth) | Not applicable |

The AMS figures above come from the report's price tables as summarized by IndexBox, because the tables in the AMS PDF did not come through as text when we opened it. Check the [current AMS report](https://www.ams.usda.gov/mnreports/lsmngfbeef.pdf) for the latest numbers, which come out each quarter.

On ground beef, the most common purchase, the grass-fed premium works out to roughly $4 to $5 a pound, or about 60 percent. For a household that eats 2 pounds of ground beef a week, that is about $400 to $550 more a year. Prices also vary a lot by region and by farm, so always confirm with the farm before you budget.

## Why it costs more

The price gap comes mostly from how long the animals take to grow and how much meat each one yields. An Oklahoma State University Extension fact sheet puts grass-finished cattle at harvest at 20 to 26 months old, compared with 14 to 22 months for cattle finished on grain in a feedlot. The same fact sheet lists a typical grass-finished carcass at about 638 pounds, against about 832 pounds for a grain-finished one. So a grass-finished animal eats and occupies land for longer and gives roughly a quarter less beef.

Most grass-fed beef is also sold by small farms that pay for small-scale processing, storage and shipping. That explains why direct-sale prices, like those in the AMS report, sit well above supermarket prices.

## What the extra money buys

### A different diet for the animal

This is the one thing you can count on, if the label is honest. "Grass-fed" on a US label does not always mean the animal ate only grass for its whole life. Some brands use "grass-fed" for cattle that were finished on grain. If you want cattle that ate grass and forage until slaughter, look for the words "grass-finished" or "100% grass-fed," and ideally a third-party certification. Our guide to [grass-fed vs grass-finished](/guides/grass-fed-vs-grass-finished/) explains the label rules, and the [certifications page](/certifications/) shows what each program checks.

### Leaner meat, with yellower fat

A 2008 study in the *Journal of Animal Science* by Leheska and colleagues sampled beef from 15 grass-fed producers in 13 states and compared it with conventional beef from three regions. Grass-fed strip steaks had less fat (2.8 percent versus 4.4 percent of raw weight), though both were lean. For ground beef the difference (12.8 versus 14.7 percent) was not statistically meaningful. The grass-fed steaks also had yellower fat, which the authors linked to carotene from forage or to the fat's makeup. If you see yellow fat on a grass-fed steak, that is normal.

Leaner meat cooks differently. The American Grassfed Association, a certifier, says grass-fed beef cooks about 30 percent faster than grain-fed beef. It suggests using a meat thermometer, taking the meat off the heat about 10 degrees before your target and lowering oven temperatures by 50 degrees when roasting. If you overcook a grass-fed steak the way you would a fattier grocery steak, it may seem tough, and you may decide it was not worth the money.

### More omega-3, in small absolute amounts

Omega-3s are a family of fats. Fish contain the long-chain kinds called EPA and DHA; plants and grass contain a shorter one called ALA.

In the Leheska study, omega-3 fats made up 0.88 percent of the fat in grass-fed ground beef against 0.24 percent in conventional ground beef, and 1.07 versus 0.19 percent in strip steaks. That sounds like a big jump, and in relative terms it is. But beef has little fat to begin with, so the absolute amounts are small. Using the study's own fat and fatty acid figures, our rough estimate per 100 grams of raw meat (about 3.5 ounces) is:

| Food, 100 g raw | Omega-3 per 100 g (approximate) | Main type |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional strip steak | about 8 mg | Mostly ALA |
| Grass-fed strip steak | about 30 mg | Mostly ALA, some DPA |
| Conventional ground beef | about 35 mg | Mostly ALA |
| Grass-fed ground beef | about 110 mg | Mostly ALA, some DPA |
| Farmed Atlantic salmon | about 1,900 mg of EPA plus DHA alone | EPA and DHA |

The salmon figure comes from USDA nutrient data as listed by University of Idaho Extension. Even the higher grass-fed number is a small fraction of what salmon gives you, and the beef's omega-3 is mostly ALA rather than EPA and DHA. In the Leheska data, EPA was barely present and DHA was below the level the lab could measure in both kinds of beef. For scale, the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements lists 1.1 grams of ALA a day for adult women and 1.6 grams for men. By our estimate, 100 grams of grass-fed ground beef supplies under a tenth of that.

The study authors themselves said the possible effects of these differences on human health cannot be judged without more research. So if omega-3 is your main reason for buying grass-fed beef, the premium buys you relatively little of it.

### Results vary from farm to farm

A larger study published in the *Journal of Animal Science* (Varre and colleagues, summarized by the American Society of Animal Science in March 2026) tested more than 300 beef samples from North American farms and supermarkets. On average, grass-fed beef had more omega-3 and more of some minerals (iron, calcium, copper and selenium) than grain-fed beef. But the researchers found large variation within both groups. Some grain-fed samples matched or beat grass-fed ones, and grass-fed beef from pastures with fewer plant species tended to score lower. The biggest differences showed up when cattle were moved between paddocks (rotational grazing) on pastures with many plant species. The authors suggested that labels showing measured omega-3 content would tell shoppers more than "grass-fed" or "grain-fed" alone.

In plain terms: the word on the label is a starting point, not a guarantee. Asking a farm how its cattle graze tells you more. Our [guide to vetting a farm](/guides/how-to-vet-a-grass-fed-farm/) lists the questions.

## What the extra money does not clearly buy

**A smaller climate footprint.** Studies disagree, and many find the opposite. The Oklahoma State fact sheet reports that published estimates put grain-finished beef's carbon footprint per unit of beef 18.5 to 67.5 percent lower than grass-finished beef's, partly because grass-finished cattle live longer and give less meat. The same fact sheet notes that counting carbon stored in pasture soil could lower the grass-finished footprint by 42 percent in one cited study, and that grass-finished cattle eat forage people cannot eat. A West Coast study by Klopatek and colleagues found the conventional system had the lowest greenhouse gas footprint but the highest energy use, while a 20-month grass-fed system used the least water. The authors concluded no single system came out ahead on economics, meat quality and environment together. If land practices matter to you, ask the farm what it actually does rather than relying on "grass-fed" alone.

**Better taste, guaranteed.** Flavor is personal. Grass-fed beef is leaner and its fat can taste different, and some people prefer grain-finished beef. If you are unsure, buy a pound of ground beef and a steak from one farm before committing to a large order.

## How to pay less for grass-fed beef

**Buy a share of an animal.** In the AMS report, a whole, half or quarter share averaged $11.90 to $13.30 per pound of meat you take home, and that price covers steaks and roasts as well as ground beef. Compare that with $26 to $29 a pound for strip and ribeye steaks bought by the cut. You need freezer space and cash up front: the same report lists average deposits of about $262 for a quarter and $449 for a half. Our guides to [buying a quarter cow](/guides/how-to-buy-a-quarter-cow/) and the [bulk beef pages](/bulk-beef/) cover the details.

**Split a share.** If a quarter is too much meat, two households can split it. You still get the bulk price per pound.

**Buy the cheaper cuts by the pound.** In the AMS data, regular ground beef averaged $10.78 a pound and chuck or shoulder roasts $12.84, compared with $26 to $41 for steaks. Mixing grass-fed ground beef and roasts with grocery beef for other meals is one way to keep the cost manageable.

**Buy local.** Shipping adds cost to mail-order beef. A farm near you may sell for less and let you pick up. You can [search farms by state](/farms/) on our directory.

## So, is it worth it?

It is worth it if you value knowing how and where your beef was raised, and you buy from a farm that can tell you clearly that its cattle were grass-finished. It is harder to justify on nutrition alone, because the omega-3 difference is small next to fish and varies a lot from farm to farm. If price is the sticking point, a bulk share brings grass-fed beef much closer to grocery prices per pound of mixed cuts. Whatever you decide, confirm current prices and feeding practices with the farm before you order.
