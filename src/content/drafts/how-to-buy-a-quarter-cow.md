A quarter cow is one fourth of a beef carcass, bought straight from a farm and cut to your instructions by a local butcher. You usually pay the farm a price per pound of hanging weight, then pay the butcher for slaughter and cutting, and you take home roughly 60 to 70 percent of the hanging weight as packaged meat. For a grass-finished quarter, plan on about 100 to 140 pounds of beef and about 3 to 5 cubic feet of freezer space.

## Quarter, half and whole shares

Farms sell bulk beef in three main sizes:

- **Whole:** the entire carcass. Oklahoma State University Extension puts an average carcass at about 600 pounds hanging weight.
- **Half (also called a side):** one side of the carcass, split down the backbone. Oklahoma State says a side usually weighs a little over 300 pounds.
- **Quarter:** half of a side. Traditionally this meant either a **forequarter** (chuck, rib, brisket, plate) or a **hindquarter** (round, loin, flank). The hindquarter has more steaks and costs more per pound; the forequarter has more pot roasts and costs less.

Some farms sell a mixed quarter instead, with cuts from each end. The University of Minnesota Extension example of a quarter includes ribeyes and T-bones as well as round steaks, roasts and 35 to 60 pounds of ground beef. Ask the farm which kind of quarter it sells before you pay a deposit.

| Share | Typical hanging weight | Typical take-home meat | Freezer space (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quarter | 150 to 200 lb | 100 to 140 lb | 3 to 4.5 cu ft |
| Half | 300 to 400 lb | 200 to 280 lb | 5 to 8 cu ft |
| Whole | 600 to 800 lb | 400 to 560 lb | 10 to 16 cu ft |

These are rough ranges built from the extension figures in this guide (carcass weights of about 600 to 830 pounds, a 65 to 70 percent bone-in cutting yield and about 35 to 40 pounds of meat per cubic foot). Your animal can fall outside them.

## Hanging weight vs. take-home weight

Three weights matter, and farms quote different ones:

1. **Live weight** is the animal on the hoof.
2. **Hanging weight** (also called carcass or hot weight) is what is left after slaughter, once the hide, head, feet and organs are removed. The University of Georgia Extension gives a typical range of 58 to 64 percent of live weight.
3. **Take-home weight** (also called packaged or net weight) is the cut, trimmed and wrapped meat you put in your freezer.

The drop from hanging to take-home weight is the one that surprises buyers. A carcass is 70 to 75 percent water and loses 2 to 5 percent of its weight while it chills and ages, according to University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension. Then the butcher removes bone and fat. Ohio State University Extension gives a cutting yield of 65 to 70 percent of hanging weight for bone-in beef and 55 to 60 percent for boneless. Oklahoma State puts cutting losses anywhere from 24 to 45 percent, depending on how fat the carcass is.

Your own choices move the number. Wisconsin Extension notes that boneless cuts, closely trimmed steaks and lean ground beef (such as 90 percent lean) all lower your take-home pounds, while keeping the liver, heart or tongue adds to them. The edible meat is the same either way; you are just paying to have the bone removed.

Grass-finished cattle tend to be smaller. An Oklahoma State fact sheet compares a grass-finished steer with a carcass of about 638 pounds to a grain-finished steer at about 832 pounds. That means a grass-finished quarter often lands near 160 pounds hanging, or roughly 100 to 110 pounds in the freezer.

## How pricing works

Most bulk beef is priced in three parts:

- **The beef itself**, paid to the farm, usually as a price per pound of hanging weight. Oklahoma State Extension tells buyers that the price is based on hanging weight and to compare farms on that basis.
- **Processing (cut and wrap)**, usually a price per pound of hanging weight, paid to the butcher.
- **A slaughter or kill fee**, a flat charge per animal that the share owners split.

Some farms roll processing into one price, and some price by live weight or by the head instead. Oregon State University Extension says custom-exempt sales (explained below) are priced on live weight or per head, while most other extension guides describe hanging-weight pricing. Ask exactly which weight you are paying on and what is included.

### What it costs (2025 data)

The most recent national figures we found come from the USDA Agricultural Marketing Service, which tracks prices for beef sold direct to consumers. Its quarterly report for July to September 2025 listed:

| Item (paid by the consumer) | Range | Average |
|---|---|---|
| Quarter, per lb hanging weight, paid to producer, processing not included | $4.50 to $8.00 | $6.21 |
| Half, per lb hanging weight, processing not included | $4.25 to $6.95 | $5.75 |
| Whole, per lb hanging weight, processing not included | $4.00 to $6.75 | $5.61 |
| Processing, per lb hanging weight, paid to processor | $0.57 to $1.25 | $0.94 |
| Slaughter fee, per head | $100 to $150 | $125 |

These numbers sit in the general direct-to-consumer section of the report, not the grass-fed section, so treat them as a starting point. A grass-finished farm may charge more or less. Prices also vary by region and year, so confirm the current price with the farm.

### A worked example

Using the 2025 USDA averages and a 160 pound grass-finished quarter:

- Beef: 160 lb × $6.21 = about $994
- Processing: 160 lb × $0.94 = about $150
- Your quarter of a $125 slaughter fee: about $31
- **Total: about $1,175**

If you take home 105 pounds, that works out to about $11.20 per pound across every cut, from ground beef to ribeyes. That per-pound-of-meat number is the one to compare with store prices. Oklahoma State Extension also suggests counting the cost of running the freezer.

Extras such as patties or stew meat can cost more per pound, so ask for the processor's price list.

## How to fill in a cut sheet

A cut sheet (or cutting instructions) is the form that tells the butcher how to cut your share. With custom-exempt beef, Oregon State Extension says the buyer usually calls the cut-and-wrap shop directly. If you are splitting a half with someone, you may have to agree on one set of instructions.

The main choices, using a typical processor form as a guide:

- **Steak thickness and count.** Three quarters of an inch is a common default. Thicker steaks mean fewer of them. Choose how many steaks go in each package for your household.
- **Roast size.** Around 2.5 to 3 pounds is average. You can also turn roasts into ground beef or stew meat.
- **Ground beef.** Pick package size (often 1, 1.5 or 2 pound packages) and whether you want any patties.
- **Chuck.** Roasts, ground, or a mix. Ask whether the flat iron steak is saved.
- **Rib.** Bone-in ribeyes, boneless ribeyes or a standing rib roast.
- **Short loin.** T-bones and porterhouses, or New York strips plus filets. You cannot have both: the T-bone contains the strip and the filet.
- **Sirloin and round.** Sirloin steaks, tri-tip, round steaks, roasts, cube steak or ground.
- **Brisket, short ribs, shanks, flank and skirt.** Keep them whole or send them to ground.
- **Organs and bones.** Liver, heart, tongue, oxtail, soup bones and marrow bones are usually yours if you ask.

On aging (letting the carcass hang in a cooler before cutting), the same Kentucky form lists an average hang time of 12 to 21 days and does not promise an exact number. If aging matters to you, ask the butcher how long they hang beef.

A simple rule of thumb from Oklahoma State: by weight, a carcass is roughly one quarter waste, one quarter ground and stew meat, one quarter steaks and one quarter roasts. If your family mostly eats ground beef, sending more cuts to ground is normal.

## Freezer space

Oklahoma State Extension says to allow one cubic foot of freezer space for every 35 to 40 pounds of cut and wrapped beef, and more if the packages are oddly shaped. University of Minnesota Extension recommends at least 4.5 cubic feet in a chest freezer, or 5.5 cubic feet in an upright, for a quarter of about 142 pounds.

Storage time matters too. Oklahoma State gives 9 to 12 months for beef cuts and 3 to 4 months for ground beef for best quality. A quarter that a small household works through in a year fits that window better than a half.

## Timing and seasonality

Bulk beef is not an on-demand purchase. Expect a wait, sometimes months:

- **The animal has to be ready.** Oklahoma State reports grass-finished cattle are typically harvested at about 20 to 26 months old, older than grain-finished cattle, and that finishing depends on the forage available. A short northern growing season can mean lighter animals. Ask the farm when its next animals will be ready.
- **The butcher has to have a slot.** Cornell's Small Farms Program advises calling the processor well ahead of the slaughter date you want.
- **Shares sell before slaughter.** With custom-exempt processing, Oregon State Extension says every share must be sold before the animal is slaughtered. That is why farms take deposits early.
- **Aging and cutting take time.** Add the hang time plus a few days to cut, wrap and freeze.

### Custom-exempt vs. USDA-inspected

When you buy a share of a live animal, the beef is often processed at a **custom-exempt** shop. The animal is not federally inspected, and the meat is for your household only. According to Oregon State Extension, packages must be marked "not for sale" and cannot carry other claims, such as "natural" or "humane." Some farms instead use a USDA-inspected plant, and those packages carry an inspection mark. Neither type is a grass-fed guarantee by itself.

## Questions to ask the farm about grass-finishing

Ask about how the animal was finished (its diet in the last months before harvest), not just whether it ate grass. Under draft FSIS (USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service) guidance from 2016, as summarized by the American Grassfed Association, a grass-fed label claim means the animal ate only forage after weaning, with no grain, and had continuous pasture access during the growing season. In August 2024, FSIS said it strongly encourages third-party certification to back up animal-raising claims. Because custom-exempt packages carry no such claims, your assurance is what the farm tells you and any certification it holds, so ask:

1. **Was the animal grass-fed and grass-finished, with no grain at any point after weaning?**
2. **What does it eat in winter or during a drought?** The FSIS definition excludes grain and grain by-products, so ask whether any were fed, even briefly.
3. **Is the farm certified grass-fed, and by whom?** See our [certifications guide](/certifications/) for what each label requires.
4. **How old will the animal be at harvest, and what is the expected hanging weight?**
5. **What is the price per pound, on which weight, and what does it include?**
6. **Who is the processor, is it custom-exempt or USDA-inspected, and how do I submit my cut sheet?**
7. **How long does the processor age the beef?**
8. **What deposit is due, when is the balance due, and when and where do I pick up?**
