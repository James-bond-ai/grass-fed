White Oak Pastures is a family farm in Bluffton, Georgia, that raises beef, lamb, goat, pork and poultry and ships frozen meat across much of the US. It says all of its cattle are "grass-fed and grass-finished," processes them in its own on-farm plant, and names several outside labels, including the American Grassfed Association. On November 13, 2026, its ground beef was $14.99 a pound, single steaks ran about $27 to $96 a pound, and its quarter and half cow boxes worked out to about $18 to $19 a pound.

## How we put this review together

Everything here comes from White Oak Pastures' own website and blog, read on November 13, 2026, plus a few outside pages: a University of Georgia profile of the owner, two certifier pages and the Savory Institute's summary of a carbon study. We did not order, cook or taste anything. Some pages are years old; we flag those dates. Prices change often, so check before you buy.

## Who they are

The Harris family has farmed the same land in Bluffton, in southwest Georgia, since 1866, according to a University of Georgia profile of owner Will Harris. The same profile says Harris took over in the early 1990s and moved the farm away from a conventional model: he stopped tilling, stopped using chemical fertilizer and pesticides, and stopped giving routine low-dose antibiotics.

Today the farm says it raises ten species of livestock using Holistic Management, a planned grazing method where animals are moved often so pastures can rest and regrow. It also grows organic vegetables, runs a General Store in Bluffton, rents cabins and offers farm tours. You can see its basic details in [our directory listing](/farm/white-oak-pastures/).

## How the cattle are raised

The farm's FAQ says its cattle, sheep and goats graze pasture year-round and are 100% grassfed. In the warm months the pastures are perennial grasses such as bermudagrass and bahiagrass. In winter the farm overseeds them with cool-season plants like ryegrass and clover so there is still something green to graze. The FAQ says the cattle are at least about 24 months old at slaughter and are bred naturally rather than by artificial insemination.

The farm says it uses no hormones or antibiotics. It does vaccinate cattle, sheep and goats against several common diseases, which is normal practice.

"Grass-fed" alone only promises that an animal ate grass at some point. "Grass-finished" means it kept eating grass and forage right up to slaughter, with no grain at the end. Our guide to [grass-fed vs grass-finished](/guides/grass-fed-vs-grass-finished/) explains the gap. White Oak Pastures uses the stronger wording for its beef, and its certifications page says some of its cattle are born on the pastures of a local producer group before they come to the farm. If you want beef born and raised on one farm, ask about that before you order.

Not every animal on the farm eats only grass, and the FAQ says so:

| Animal | What the farm says it eats |
|---|---|
| Cattle, sheep, goats | Pasture year-round, 100% grassfed |
| Hogs | Pasture, cracked eggs and blemished vegetables from the farm, plus a free-choice non-GMO grain mix (corn, soy, wheat) |
| Chickens and other poultry | Pasture and insects, plus a non-GMO grain mix (corn, soy, barley) |

Pigs and poultry can't live on grass alone, so this is expected. Just don't assume the pork or chicken is grass-fed because the beef is.

## On-farm processing

White Oak Pastures slaughters its own animals on the farm. Its "Our farm" page says only one other US beef farm, in California, has a USDA-inspected plant on the property. Its animal welfare page says the red meat plant opened in 2008 and the poultry plant in 2011, both USDA-inspected and both designed by animal scientist Temple Grandin. The farm says animals are held in open pens and stunned one at a time with a hand-held captive bolt.

The processing page says the farm slaughters 25 cattle a day, while the "Our farm" page says 30. Either way, it is small next to commercial plants. The farm says it composts or reuses almost every part of the animal: blood and bones become fertilizer, hides become leather or pet chews, and fat goes into soaps and candles.

## Certifications

White Oak Pastures' certifications page lists these labels for its animals:

| Animals | Labels the farm says it holds |
|---|---|
| Cattle | American Grassfed Association (AGA) Certified Grassfed, Certified Humane, Global Animal Partnership Step 4, Ecological Outcome Verified (EOV) |
| Sheep, goats | AGA Certified Grassfed, Certified Humane, EOV |
| Hogs | Certified Non-GMO, Certified Humane, EOV |
| Chickens, turkeys, laying hens | Certified Non-GMO, Certified Humane, EOV |
| Vegetables | Certified organic |

For beef shoppers, the AGA label is the one that speaks to feed: it is a grass-fed standard, while Certified Humane and Global Animal Partnership are welfare programs. EOV is a Savory Institute program that measures changes in soil and plant life on the land rather than how animals are fed. Our [certifications page](/certifications/) explains what each program checks.

We could not confirm every label in a certifier's current listing during this review. Will Harris appears on the American Grassfed Association's board of directors page, but that page is not a certification listing. Certified Humane's pages about the farm are a 2005 announcement and a 2014 article, not a current directory entry. In our October 2026 check of the USDA Organic Integrity Database, the farm's record was certified for crops only, which matches the farm's own statement that it does not use the organic label for its meat. If a specific label matters to you, ask the farm for a current certificate.

The bundle listings page showed the 1/8 cow at $605.30, but we could not open its product page, so we don't know its weight or contents.

About half of the 1/4 cow box by weight is ground beef: 30 one-pound packs, plus steaks, roasts, stew meat, stir-fry strips and short ribs. The product pages say you need about 4 cubic feet of freezer space for the quarter and 8 for the half.

These boxes are different from the quarter or half cow you'd buy from a local rancher. A local share is usually priced on hanging weight, lets you fill out a cut sheet and often yields much more meat. ## The carbon study

White Oak Pastures often points to a 2019 life cycle assessment by the consulting firm Quantis, based on 2017 farm data. The farm's blog says the study found its soils store more carbon than its grassfed beef herd emits. The Savory Institute's summary notes that the study only counts emissions up to slaughter, so packaging, shipping, storage and cooking are left out. It is one study of one farm, and the frozen shipping that gets the meat to your door is outside its scope. Treat it as the farm's own reported result, not a general rule about grass-fed beef.

## Who it suits, and who it doesn't

White Oak Pastures may suit you if:

- You want beef described as grass-finished from a named US farm, with processing done on the same property.
- You'd like a claimed AGA grass-fed label and welfare certification on the same product.
- You want lamb, goat or less common cuts and organ meats alongside beef.
- You live in the Southeast, where ground shipping reaches you, or near Atlanta.

It may not suit you if:

- You want the lowest price per pound. A share from a local ranch in our [farm directory](/farms/) often costs less; our guide on [how to vet a grass-fed farm](/guides/how-to-vet-a-grass-fed-farm/) has questions to ask any seller.
- You live on the West Coast and don't want to pay express shipping.
- You want to choose your own cuts in a cow share.
- You need every label confirmed in a certifier's current directory. Ask the farm for certificates.

## Before you order

1. Add up your order. Aim for more than $249 to get free ground shipping.
2. Check whether your address falls inside the ground shipping zone before you build a big order.
3. Clear freezer space: about 4 cubic feet for a quarter box, 8 for a half.
4. Ask for current certificates if a specific label is the reason you're buying.

## The bottom line

White Oak Pastures states clearly that its cattle are grass-finished, slaughters them on its own farm and names more outside labels than most mail-order sellers. You pay for that: about $15 a pound for ground beef and $18 to $19 a pound for its preset cow boxes on the day we looked. Our [grass-fed beef delivery comparison](/guides/best-grass-fed-beef-delivery/) shows how that stacks up against other mail-order sellers. Read each product page, check your shipping zone and confirm current prices with the farm.
