There is no federal definition of grass-fed for milk or butter, so the words on the carton mean whatever the brand, or the certifier it pays to inspect it, says they mean. Among the national brands we looked at, the strictest claims (no grain at all, checked by a third party every year) come with a seal from OPT, the American Grassfed Association or A Greener World. Brands that say "grass-fed" without a seal may still feed some grain, and some say so openly.

## Why grass-fed dairy labels vary so much

USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service (AMS) once had a voluntary grass-fed marketing standard. It withdrew it in January 2016, saying the standard did not help farmers or shoppers and that label approval sits with other agencies anyway: the Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) for meat and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for products like milk and butter. That leaves two kinds of grass-fed dairy claim on store shelves:

- **Certified claims.** An independent group writes a standard, inspects the farms and lets the brand print its seal. You can read the standard yourself.
- **Brand claims.** The company describes its own feeding in its own words. Some are detailed and specific; some are not. Either way, nobody outside the company has to check them.

USDA Organic sits underneath many grass-fed dairy products, but on its own it is a pasture rule, not a grass-fed rule. Certified organic cows must graze for the whole grazing season, at least 120 days a year, and get on average at least 30 percent of their dry matter intake (the weight of feed once the water is removed) from grazing during that season. The rule requires organic feed but does not ban grain. Organic Valley, for example, says its regular organic cows eat on average about 85 percent grass and forage and 15 percent organic grain, which is why it doesn't call that milk grass-fed.

For a full comparison of every grass-fed label, including the beef ones, see [grass-fed certifications compared](/guides/grass-fed-certifications-compared/) and our [certifications page](/certifications/).

## The three grass-fed seals you'll see on dairy

| Seal | Grain allowed? | Pasture rule | Must also be organic? | Who checks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OPT Certified Grass-Fed Organic | No | At least 60% of dry matter intake from pasture over a grazing season of at least 150 days (2019 launch figures) | Yes | Accredited organic certifiers, every year |
| American Grassfed Association (AGA) | No, forage only | Animals graze pasture for most, if not all, of their nutrition | No | Independent third parties, at least every 15 months |
| Certified Grassfed by AGW | No (limited sprouted-grain fodder in drought, with approval) | Grass and forage diet for life | No, but must also hold Animal Welfare Approved | A Greener World auditors |

**OPT (Organic Plus Trust).** Organic Valley and Maple Hill launched this program in February 2019. A farm has to be certified organic first. Then the cows get a zero-grain diet, and the 2019 announcement set the pasture minimum at 60 percent of dry matter intake over at least 150 days, double the organic share and 30 days longer. Farms are certified for grass-fed every year by an independent third party, and the whole supply chain, from farm to processor, has to be certified too. See [OPT on our certifications page](/certifications/#opt).

**American Grassfed Association.** AGA's standards cover dairy cattle as well as meat animals. Its general rules allow forage only, no grain, keep animals on pasture except for short handling or emergencies, and ban antibiotics, ionophores and hormones. Farms are inspected at least every 15 months. See [AGA on our certifications page](/certifications/#aga).

**Certified Grassfed by AGW.** This is an add-on to Animal Welfare Approved (AWA), a welfare certification from A Greener World. The 2024 standards ban grain, grain by-products and other feed concentrates. The one exception is sprouted grain fodder during drought or emergency, capped at 25 percent of daily dry matter intake and only with prior approval. For cow dairies, a farm must hold AWA for at least one audit cycle and feed 100 percent grass for at least a year before it can certify, and it must test its forage. See [Certified Grassfed by AGW](/certifications/#agw-grassfed).

## Grass-fed milk and butter brands compared

We checked each brand's own website (and press releases where the website was silent) on November 16, 2026. We list only the certifications a brand or certifier names; we did not taste, buy or test any product. Labels change, so check the seal on the carton in front of you.

| Brand and product | Grass-fed wording | Grain in the diet | Seal or standard named | Who verifies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic Valley Grassmilk | Cows fed fresh grass and dried forage, plus small supplements such as molasses | None | Certified Grassfed by OPT, USDA Organic | OPT and organic certifiers |
| Maple Hill milk and dairy | "100% grass-fed" | None stated | Website names none; PCO 100% grass-fed in 2014, co-founded OPT in 2019 | Check the carton |
| Kalona SuperNatural kefir | 100% grass-fed, forage only | None, per AGA | AGA (two kefir products announced in 2017) | AGA auditors |
| Kerrygold butter | Irish cows, on average 95% grass or grass forage by fresh weight | Small amount of supplementary feed | Ireland's Grass Fed Dairy Standard | Irish scheme audits |
| Vital Farms butter | "90% grass-fed" | About 10%: barley, oats, corn meal, vitamins and minerals | None named on the butter page | Company statement |

### Organic Valley Grassmilk

Grassmilk is Organic Valley's 100 percent grass-fed line, separate from its regular organic milk. The product page says the cows eat fresh grass and dried forage, with a small amount of supplements such as molasses and no grain. The carton carries the Certified Grassfed by OPT seal and USDA Organic. Organic Valley says Grassmilk farms were among the first certified under OPT, with the seal added to cartons in late 2019. The Cornucopia Institute, an organic watchdog group, gives Grassmilk full marks on its grass-fed criterion. One detail differs between sources: Cornucopia's scorecard says no legumes are fed, while Organic Valley's own pages describe cows eating legumes such as clover and alfalfa as part of their forage. Neither changes the no-grain point.

Organic Valley's other milk and butter come from organic cows that eat some grain, so only products marked Grassmilk or carrying the OPT seal meet the grass-only standard.

### Maple Hill

Maple Hill sells milk, yogurt and other dairy and says all of it is made with 100 percent grass-fed milk from pasture-raised cows; its milk is listed with organic milk as the ingredient. In 2014 Pennsylvania Certified Organic (PCO) certified Maple Hill under a new 100 percent grass-fed organic standard that banned grain and grain by-products and required yearly inspection and feed records. In 2019 Maple Hill and Organic Valley launched the OPT program described above. Maple Hill's current milk page, read in November 2026, does not name a grass-fed certifier, so look for the OPT or PCO mark on the package you buy.

### Kalona SuperNatural

Kalona SuperNatural is an organic dairy brand. In August 2017, AGA announced that two Kalona products, plain and vanilla organic whole milk kefir, would carry the AGA grass-fed seal from January 2018. The announcement describes the standard as pasture grazing and 100 percent forage with no grain, plus bans on confinement, growth hormones and antibiotics, verified by a yearly on-farm audit. That announcement covered the kefir only. It doesn't tell you whether Kalona's milk or butter is certified grass-fed, so check for the AGA seal on each product.

### Kerrygold

Kerrygold butter comes from Ireland. Its US website says all dairy suppliers for US Kerrygold products are certified to Ireland's Grass Fed Dairy Standard, which it describes as a diet that is on average 95 percent grass or grass forage. Two details matter. First, that figure is measured by fresh weight, while US organic and OPT rules count dry matter, so the percentages aren't directly comparable. Second, Kerrygold says the cows also get a small amount of supplementary feed, and in winter they eat stored grass as silage, haylage or hay.

Kerrygold's websites also disagree a little. The UK site says cows graze outdoors up to 300 days a year and that every Irish farm is independently audited every 18 months under a national dairy assurance scheme. The US FAQ says Irish farmers "may participate" in that scheme. The grass-fed standard is the claim the US site ties to every supplier.

### Vital Farms butter

Vital Farms is clear about grain. Its butter page says the cows are 90 percent grass-fed, and an April 2024 company announcement said the other 10 percent of their diet is barley, oats, corn meal and supplemental vitamins and minerals. The cows have access to pasture during the warm months, which the company puts at an average of 200 days a year. The butter page names no grass-fed certifier. If grain-free matters to you, this butter doesn't meet that bar, but the company tells you so plainly.

## How to read a grass-fed dairy label in the store

1. **Look for a seal first.** OPT, AGA or Certified Grassfed by AGW each mean a no-grain diet rule (AGW allows a narrow drought exception, as the table shows) and outside inspection. A plain "grass-fed" with no seal is the brand's own claim.
2. **Look for a number.** "100%" or "grass-fed only" says more than "grass-fed." A brand that gives a percentage, like 90 or 95 percent, is telling you some of the diet isn't grass.
3. **Don't treat organic as grass-fed.** USDA Organic guarantees pasture time and organic feed, not a grain-free diet.
4. **Check each product, not the brand.** Organic Valley and Kalona both sell some products with a grass-fed seal and others without one under the same name.
5. **Check the brand's website.** Several brands explain their feeding in detail online. If a brand won't say what else the cows eat, ask.

## Buying grass-fed milk and butter from a farm

Small dairy farms often sell milk, butter and cheese from their own herds, and you can ask them directly what the cows eat in winter. Our [grass-fed dairy directory](/dairy/) lists farms by state. A few examples: [Coulter Farms](/farm/coulter-farms/) in Pennsylvania and [Larson Farm](/farm/larson-farm/) in Vermont are certified organic and describe their cows as 100 percent grass-fed, and both sell butter. [North Country Creamery](/farm/north-country-creamery/) in New York holds Certified Grassfed by AGW. [Nordic Creamery](/farm/nordic-creamery/) in Wisconsin ships butter nationwide and says its cows graze in summer and eat mostly grass hay in winter.

Some farms sell raw (unpasteurized) milk, which is legal only in some states and under different rules in each. See [raw milk laws by state](/guides/raw-milk-laws-by-state/) before you buy. For the questions worth asking any farm, see [how to vet a grass-fed farm](/guides/how-to-vet-a-grass-fed-farm/).
